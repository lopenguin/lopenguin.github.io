The moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy generalizes the Lagrangian

ⓘ See Also
This post draws from Prof. Davis Knowles's notes on the Lagrangian and the sum-of-squares formalisms in Nie and Demmel (2005).

There's a deep, somewhat subtle connection between Lagrangian duality and the moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy for polynomial optimization. In this post I will show, for polynomial optimization problems, that the sum-of-squares hierarchy essentially generalizes the Lagrangian dual to a polynomial dual.

Setup

Consider a general polynomial optimization problem:

p⋆=min⁡xf(x)s.t. gi(x)≤0, i=1,...,N,\begin{aligned} p^\star = \min_{x} &f(x)\\ \mathrm{s.t.}\:&g_i(x) \leq 0,\ i=1,...,N, \end{aligned}p⋆=xmin​s.t.​f(x)gi​(x)≤0, i=1,...,N,​

where fff and gig_igi​ are polynomials of order at most ddd in x∈Rnx\in\mathbb{R}^nx∈Rn.

This problem is non-convex for generic polynomials. Instead of trying to solve it exactly, we'll focus on two relaxation approaches. The first is Lagrangian duality, the standard approach, which imposes a linear penalty on constraint violation. In this post we show the moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy generalizes the linear penality to a polynomial penalty to achieve better bounds.

Preview

Before we get to the proofs, let me introduce duality informally. We want to solve a constrained optimization problem like (1). We know how to solve unconstrained problems, so a natural idea is to convert (1) into an unconstrained problem. What if we penalize constraint violation by an infinite amount?

p⋆=min⁡xf(x)+∑i=1NI(gi(x)),\begin{aligned} p^\star = \min_{x} &f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N I(g_i(x)), \end{aligned}p⋆=xmin​​f(x)+i=1∑N​I(gi​(x)),​

This does what we want, but it is non-smooth and thus hard to optimize. The Lagrangian approach replaces I(u)I(u)I(u) with J(u)=λuJ(u) = \lambda uJ(u)=λu; a straight line! Its behavior is exactly the same as the infinite step function if we first maximize over the parameter λ\lambdaλ:

The Lagrangian dual simply swaps the min and max in the equation above, giving a convex problem which lower bounds p⋆p^\starp⋆. The lower-bound result holds because the linear penalty J(u)J(u)J(u) is a lower bound on the "true" infinite penalty I(u)I(u)I(u).

I'm using a compact SOS notation, but this is the standard Lagrangian dual and moment-sos-hierarchy of order κ\kappaκ. Lagrangian dual

In this section, we show the Lagrangian dual gives a lower bound to (1) and imposes a linear penalty on constraint violation. Eq. (1) is equivalent to:

p⋆=min⁡xmax⁡λ∈R≥0Nf(x)+∑i=1Nλigi(x). p^\star = \min_x \max_{\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^N_{\geq 0}} f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i g_i(x). p⋆=xmin​λ∈R≥0N​max​f(x)+i=1∑N​λi​gi​(x).

The new objective is called the Lagrangian. To see the equivalence with (1), consider the minimum and maximum together. If any of the constraints on xxx are not satisfied, the maximum gives +∞+\infty+∞ which cannot be minimized. Thus, any finite solution requires all constraints to be satisfied. When all gi(x)≤0g_i(x) \leq 0gi​(x)≤0, the inner maximization is solved for λi≡0\lambda_i \equiv 0λi​≡0, yielding exactly (1).

The Lagrangian dual simply swaps the minimum and maximum. That is,

d⋆=max⁡λ∈R≥0Nmin⁡xf(x)+∑i=1Nλigi(x). d^\star = \max_{\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^N_{\geq 0}} \min_x f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i g_i(x). d⋆=λ∈R≥0N​max​xmin​f(x)+i=1∑N​λi​gi​(x).

Why is this dual useful? It's a concave program! The inner optimization problem is concave (more precisely, linear) in λi\lambda_iλi​. And the pointwise minimum (over all points xxx) of concave functions is concave (though it may no longer be linear). We'll make this precise in the next section. Taking the maximum of a concave function lies in the regime of convex optimization.

Importantly, the dual also provides a lower bound on the solution to (1). Consider the Lagrangian:

L(x,λ)≜f(x)+∑i=1Nλigi(x). \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) \triangleq f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i g_i(x). L(x,λ)≜f(x)+i=1∑N​λi​gi​(x).

For any primal feasible xxx (satisfying all inequality constraints) and dual feasible λ≥0\lambda \geq 0λ≥0, we have the following two inequalities:

λigi(x)≤0 ⟹ L(x,λ)≤f(x), \lambda_i g_i(x) \leq 0 \implies \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) \leq f(x), λi​gi​(x)≤0⟹L(x,λ)≤f(x),

and

min⁡xL(x,λ)≤L(x,λ). \min_x \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) \leq \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda). xmin​L(x,λ)≤L(x,λ).

Putting these together, for all feasible (x,λ)(x, \lambda)(x,λ) we have:

min⁡xL(x,λ)≤L(x,λ)≤f(x). \min_x \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) \leq \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) \leq f(x). xmin​L(x,λ)≤L(x,λ)≤f(x).

By taking the maximum over λ\lambdaλ on the lefthand side and the primal optimal xxx on the righthand side, we have shown weak dualty.

Theorem. (Weak duality) The Lagrangian dual lower bounds the primal: d⋆≤p⋆d^\star \leq p^\stard⋆≤p⋆.

Better lower bounds via moment-sum-of-squares

I've mentioned several times that the moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy replaces the scalar dual variables λi\lambda_iλi​ with polynomial duals. Let's make this explicit. Eq. (1) is equivalent to:

p⋆=min⁡xmax⁡λi∈Rκ[x]≥0f(x)+∑i=1Nλi(x)gi(x). p^\star = \min_x \max_{\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}_\kappa[x]_{\geq 0}} f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i(x) g_i(x). p⋆=xmin​λi​∈Rκ​[x]≥0​max​f(x)+i=1∑N​λi​(x)gi​(x).

The proof is exactly the same as the Lagrangian dual, noting that the notation λi(x)≥0\lambda_i(x) \geq 0λi​(x)≥0 (in fact, we can use a weaker condition that λi\lambda_iλi​ is sum-of-squares, but we introduce this later). It is perhaps unsurprising that the moment-sum-of-squares dual simply swaps the minimum and maximum:

dκ⋆=max⁡λi∈Rκ[x]≥0min⁡xf(x)+∑i=1Nλi(x)gi(x). d^\star_\kappa = \max_{\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}_\kappa[x]_{\geq 0}} \min_x f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i(x) g_i(x). dκ⋆​=λi​∈Rκ​[x]≥0​max​xmin​f(x)+i=1∑N​λi​(x)gi​(x).

The same reasoning as before gives dκ⋆≤p⋆d^\star_\kappa \leq p^\stardκ⋆​≤p⋆. The special case λi∈R≥0\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}_\geq 0λi​∈R≥​0 (all higher order terms are 000) gives d⋆≤dκ⋆d^\star \leq d^\star_\kappad⋆≤dκ⋆​. That is, the moment-sum-of-squares dual is at least as good a lower bound as the Lagrangian dual.

Theorem. (Weak polynomial duality) The polynomial dual is an upper bound on the Lagrangian dual and a lower bound on the primal: d⋆≤dκ⋆≤p⋆d^\star \leq d^\star_\kappa \leq p^\stard⋆≤dκ⋆​≤p⋆.

I want to give some intuition as to why a polynomial dual might perform better than a linear dual. Mathematically, the dual polynomial has more degrees of freedom to maximize over (this manifests as matrix comparison, which we will see below). But the idea of the Lagrangian is to move constraints into the objective with a large penalty. A polynomial dual is a much more expressive penalty than a scalar dual.

Hopefully I've convinced you that this hierarchy generalizes the Lagrangian dual using polynomial duals. We've also seen that the polynomial dual cannot give any worse of a lower bound than the Lagrangian dual. It turns out we can make a much stronger statement: under some regularity, the polynomial dual converges to the value of the (non-convex) primal as polynomial order tends towards infinity. To see this, we first need a little background in sum-of-squares programming.

Sum-of-squares background

A sum-of-squares (SOS) polynomial is simply a polynomial that can be written as the sum of the squares of other polynomials. We'll use a convenient result from Parrilo (2003) to connect SOS polynomials to positive semidefinite matrices.

Theorem. A polynomial p(x)∈R2d[x]p(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{2d}[x]p(x)∈R2d​[x] is sum-of-squares if and only if there exists A⪰0A \succeq 0A⪰0 such that p(x)=[x]dTA[xd]p(x) = [x]_d^T A [x_d]p(x)=[x]dT​A[xd​]. This can be checked with a semidefinite feasibility problem.

This representation motivates the following shorthand notation.

Notation. We write p(x)⪰sos0p(x) \succeq_{sos} 0p(x)⪰sos​0 to mean p(x)p(x)p(x) is sum-of-squares. That is, p(x)p(x)p(x) can be written as p(x)=[x]dTA[xd]p(x) = [x]_d^T A [x_d]p(x)=[x]dT​A[xd​] with A⪰0A \succeq 0A⪰0.

SOS polynomials are an important subset of the set of positive polynomials p(x)≥0p(x) \geq 0p(x)≥0. Checking whether a polynomial is positive is an NP-hard problem, but checking if p(x)p(x)p(x) is SOS only requires solving an SDP.

Fortunately, strictly positive polynomials may be represented in part with SOS polynomials. This result is called a positivestellensatz.

Theorem. (Putinar's Positivestellensatz) Let K≜{x:gi(x)≤0 ∀ i}\mathcal{K} \triangleq \{x : g_i(x) \leq 0 \ \forall\ i\}K≜{x:gi​(x)≤0 ∀ i}, where gi(x)g_i(x)gi​(x) are polynomials (a basic semialgebraic set). Assume K\mathcal{K}K is compact. If p(x)>0p(x) > 0p(x)>0 for x∈Kx\in\mathcal{K}x∈K, then

p(x)=p0(x)−∑i=1Npi(x)gi(x), p(x) = p_0(x) - \sum_{i=1}^N p_i(x) g_i(x), p(x)=p0​(x)−i=1∑N​pi​(x)gi​(x),

where pi(x)⪰sos0p_i(x) \succeq_{sos} 0pi​(x)⪰sos​0 for i=0,1,...,Ni=0,1,...,Ni=0,1,...,N.

Rearranging (25), Putinar equivalently states that there exist pi(x)⪰sos0p_i(x) \succeq_{sos} 0pi​(x)⪰sos​0 such that:

p(x)+∑i=1Npi(x)gi(x)⪰sos0. p(x) + \sum_{i=1}^N p_i(x) g_i(x) \succeq_{sos} 0. p(x)+i=1∑N​pi​(x)gi​(x)⪰sos​0.

Note that Putinar guarantees the existance of some polynomials pi(x)p_i(x)pi​(x) and does not specify order. In practice, we must restrict pi(x)p_i(x)pi​(x) to order κ\kappaκ. This leads directly to a hierarchy of relaxations which use higher-order dual polynomials.

Convergence to primal

In this section we'll show the following result, due to Lasserre (2001).

Our strategy is to squeeze the dual with a lower bound. Let x⋆x^\starx⋆ solve the primal (1) with value p⋆=f(x⋆)p^\star = f(x^\star)p⋆=f(x⋆). Since fff is a polynomial we can make δ\deltaδ arbitrarily small by appropriate choice of ϵ\epsilonϵ. This shows that with arbitrary high polynomial order the polynomial dual converges to the true value and completes the proof.

The hierarchy convergence theorem is a remarkable result because it uses convex optimization techniques to solve a non-convex problem. It's worth pointing out, however, that there's no free lunch. Stepping up the relaxation order increases the size of the problem polynomially, making high-order relaxations essentially impratical in real-world applications.

Conclusion

That was quite a journey! Duality is one of the most interesting and useful concepts in optimization, and I find it remarkable that duality can be "generalized" for a class of otherwise hard polynomial problems. The bonus of a lower bound is the possibility of a optimality gap. Given a primal feasible point xxx, the optimality gap is the difference between f(x)f(x)f(x) and the dual d⋆d^\stard⋆. There many rounding schemes to obtain a good guess for xxx from the dual solution, and this metric is the main "certificate" in certifiable optimization research.

This line of reasoning led me to a few questions for further thought:

  • Is there a generalization of the dual for other classes of problems?

    • Answer (probably): we need the dual to be convex in order to get good approximations. This greatly restricts the class of possible dual functions. Polynomials worked in this case largely because polynomials are closed under multiplication.

  • On the more practical side: are there other polynomial bases which have computational benefits?
